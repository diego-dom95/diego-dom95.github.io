The physical question

Following a neutron star’s signal.

A rotating neutron star with a small, sustained deformation can emit continuous gravitational waves. The signal may persist for months or years, but its amplitude is weak and its phase changes as the star evolves and the detector moves.

I investigate how to extract evidence from these measurements: what neural networks learn, where their sensitivity is limited, and how physical models can guide the search.

Research

Neural detection and physical inference

01

Understanding neural detection

Which information helps a network recognize a weak signal?

I study residual networks and vision transformers under common search conditions. Controlled changes to signal phase, frequency evolution, and observing schedules help explain their sensitivity and limitations.

Methods and scope

The principal comparison uses simulated signals in Gaussian noise. It evaluates complete-band detection at noise-calibrated thresholds and compares neural scores with power-based and coherent reference statistics.

A central distinction is between recognizing a frequency pattern and following the phase evolution needed for coherent integration.

02

Inference for evolving signals

How much signal can we retain when its frequency history is uncertain?

My ongoing work explores coherent and state-space searches that combine detector-response models with inference over possible signal trajectories.

Methods and scope

The investigations include path marginalization, Kalman-based amplitude inference, and learned proposals followed by physical scoring. Different methods make different assumptions about the source and the available parameters.

Known-sky studies provide a controlled setting for understanding these trade-offs before extending the search domain.

03

Reliable scientific computation

How do we make large experiments both practical and checkable?

GPU-oriented simulation supplies fresh training examples, and numerical comparisons against established tools check the physical conventions. Reproducible analysis connects the implementation to the scientific result.

Methods and scope

The workflow brings together PyTorch, complex Fourier data, LALSuite reference calculations, containerized environments, and local and supercomputer execution.

Validation is tied to explicit signal, sampling, and noise conventions. Reproducing a saved figure and repeating a training experiment are separate tasks.

About

From detector hardware to data analysis

I am a physics PhD candidate at the Institute of Science Tokyo and a JSPS Research Fellow (DC2). Before moving to Japan, I studied physics at Escuela Politécnica Nacional in Quito, Ecuador, where I also worked on water-Cherenkov detector instrumentation.

That background informs how I approach data analysis: understanding the measurement is as important as choosing the algorithm. Today I develop simulation and inference tools for gravitational-wave searches, and study the physical information that neural networks use.
